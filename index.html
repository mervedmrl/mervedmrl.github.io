About

I am a PhD candidate in Economics at the Institute for International Economic Studies (IIES), Stockholm University, and I am on the 2026–2027 academic job market.

I am an economist working in development and labor economics, with a focus on migration and education. My research examines how barriers to mobility shape access to economic opportunity and how migration affects the economic wellbeing of migrants and their families. My job market paper asks why students are willing to forgo higher expected earnings to study close to home, and tests whether mentorship from migrant peers facilitates migration.

Research

Job Market Paper

Migration Costs and Higher Education: Experimental Evidence from India

| JMP | AEA RCT Registry

Large gaps in economic opportunity across places in developing countries persist alongside low rates of migration, which a large literature attributes to the non-pecuniary costs of leaving home. This paper asks whether these costs hold prospective college students back from migrating for college, whether they reflect misperceptions, and whether access to a migrant peer can reduce perceived costs and facilitate migration. I follow 1,682 final-year secondary students across 52 schools in Maharashtra, India, over a full year as they make one of the most consequential decisions of their lives: whether and where to continue their education. I measure their perceived migration costs in an incentivized choice experiment, and run a randomized controlled trial offering half of them a phone-based mentorship program that pairs each student with a university student who migrated for college. At baseline, the average student would forgo a 129% increase in expected post-graduation earnings rather than study away from home, an amount that far exceeds the returns students expect and rises steeply with distance. Their beliefs about the difficulty of moving, safety, parental approval, and happiness away from home account for a substantial portion of perceived migration costs. Mentorship changes educational migration choices: a pre-specified educational migration index rises by 0.16 standard deviations, applications to study away from home increase by four percentage points, and migration by three percentage points. Consistent with the stated-preference evidence, these effects are driven by short-distance moves made by male students, where migration costs were lower to begin with. Mentorship improves men’s beliefs about pecuniary returns to migration, but beliefs about non-pecuniary outcomes in distant cities do not improve, suggesting that high migration costs are unlikely to be driven by misperceptions.

Working Papers

Distance, Gender, and Access to Elite Higher Education

with Xuanzhe Hou and Yash Kanoria

| Working paper

This paper supersedes an earlier version, How Far Would You Go? Higher Education and Migration Decisions in India (SSRN).

We study how migration distance shapes students' higher education choices, using administrative data from the 2015 centralized admissions to India's elite public engineering institutes. We document that candidates list programs farther from home less often and rank them lower, and that this pattern is stronger for women, who are assigned to programs 120 km closer to home than men on average. Estimating a discrete choice model of candidates' first-round decisions among the programs they can attain, we find that for a typical male candidate a program 1,000 km farther from home is worth roughly a quarter to a third of a standard deviation of program value less. Women are about 1.6 to 1.7 times as sensitive to distance as men. Holding attainable programs fixed, this difference accounts for 25 percent of the gender gap in the value of the programs candidates can attain. These findings show that spatial frictions create barriers to accessing high-quality education and widen gender inequalities in human capital accumulation, even among high-achieving students at elite STEM institutions.

Identity and Human Capital Investment: Evidence from the Veiling Ban Removal in Turkey

with Avenia Ghazarian

| Working paper

This paper examines how restrictions on religious expression affect women's educational attainment. We study the 2010 removal of the headscarf ban in Turkish universities, which had long limited access to higher education for visibly religious women. Our empirical strategy combines cohort-level variation in exposure to the reform with individual-level variation in the propensity to veil within a difference-in-differences framework. We estimate veiling propensities using an early wave of the Turkish Demographic and Health Survey and predict them for a later sample using both machine learning and parametric methods. We show that lifting the ban significantly increased educational attainment among women with a higher propensity to veil. These gains appear to be concentrated around the transition into and progression through secondary school. The results remain similar when, instead of individual-level propensities, we use pre-reform veiling prevalence at the province level as an alternative exposure measure.

Moving to Opportunity Abroad: The Effects of International Educational Migration

with Toman Barsbai, Philipp Moskopp, Marcello Perez-Alvarez, and Matthias Sutter

| AEA RCT Registry | World Bank discussion paper

This project evaluates the effects of international educational migration, a novel approach to reducing global inequality by helping individuals from low-income countries to study and work in high-income countries. We partner with Malengo, an NGO that supports Ugandan students to pursue a Bachelor's degree in Germany. We conduct a randomized controlled trial, exploiting that Malengo randomizes admission among qualified applicants, and compare the outcomes of applicants who are selected by Malengo and those who are not, as well as their families and communities in Uganda. Here, we focus on short-term outcomes within the first three years of students' arrival in Germany, i.e. before they complete their degrees and enter the labor market. We measure the effects of the intervention on objective and subjective wellbeing, cognitive skills, and aspirations of applicants and their social networks in Uganda.

Educational Migration Prospects and Human Capital Investments: Evidence from a Uganda-Germany College Access Program

with Toman Barsbai, Philipp Moskopp, Marcello Perez-Alvarez, and Matthias Sutter

| AEA RCT Registry | World Bank discussion paper

How does the opportunity of international educational migration affect human capital investments in the country of origin? We inform high school students in Uganda about a new merit-based study-abroad program and analyze how the experimentally induced changes in knowledge about the opportunity to study abroad shape their human capital investments. The Malengo program offers mentoring and financial support to young adults in Uganda who want to complete a university degree in Germany and could not otherwise afford to study at a university. Acceptance to the program combines the returns to higher education with the returns to migration and thus offers a life-changing opportunity. To be eligible, applicants must obtain a sufficiently high score in the high-stakes Uganda Advanced Certificate of Education examination. Our primary research question is whether and how educational migration prospects lead to increased human capital investments amongst students who eventually go abroad as well as those who remain in Uganda.

International Migration and Identity Formation: The Perception of the Self and Others

with Toman Barsbai, Philipp Moskopp, Marcello Perez-Alvarez, and Matthias Sutter

| AEA RCT Registry

This project examines how international migration influences identity formation, particularly in shaping the perception of the self and others. We partner with an NGO that supports secondary school graduates from Uganda with limited financial means in pursuing a bachelor's degree in Germany. Admission to the program offers students a transformative opportunity to acquire higher education and increase their earnings. At the same time, students are exposed to a vastly different environment, characterized by distinct economic systems, political institutions, cultural norms, and racial compositions. We leverage the randomized admission among shortlisted applicants and conduct a randomized controlled trial. By tracking the outcomes of applicants and their families and friends in Uganda, we aim to uncover the direct effects of international migration on the perception of the self and others, as well as the spillover effects on those who remain in the country of origin. Our primary outcomes include universalism, gender attitudes, and racial identity.
